1 · L-band & the blank

Why L-band sees water the official map can't

The official flood map uses a 5.6 cm C-band radar. Under forest it marks the area "cannot see". NISAR's 24 cm L-band wave goes through the leaves.

Rough ground scatterssome of the wave returns.
Open water is darka smooth surface sends the wave away. HH and HV both drop.
Flooded forest is brightwater + trunk form a corner reflector. HH rises, HV stays.

What we count

Only NISAR water inside the official map's blank. Water both maps see is not counted as ours.

adds = NISAR water ∩ GFM "cannot see" ∩ both valid

area = pixels × 80 m × 80 m

more = adds ÷ GFM flood

GFM = Copernicus Global Flood Monitoring (Sentinel-1). Nearest map within ±2 days, put on our 80 m grid.

"More %" is shown only where GFM maps ≥ 5 km² of flood, so tiny bases don't inflate it.

4 · Algorithm

From radar features to water

Each pixel is compared with its own dry self (its first passes, before the flood season), not with a fixed colour scale.

① Open water

HH < tHH and HV < tHV (default −17 / −24 dB)

Calm water is a mirror: the wave bounces away, so both channels go dark.

How we pick t: per site, from the data. On cloud-free days we try every pair (tHH from −21 to −11 dB in 0.5 dB steps, tHV 5–9 dB lower) and keep the one that best matches NASA's optical water map (F1). Fixed values fail: calm lake water is about −20 dB at Tonle Sap, fish ponds in Thailand about −15 dB. Used only if ≥ 2 clear days and the left-out score is ≥ 0.5; else the defaults.

② Water spreads

ΔHH and ΔHV both ≤ −2.5 dB, connected to open water within 1 km

Floods grow from rivers and lakes, so a darkening patch that touches open water is flood.

Why 2.5 dB: about half the power gone, several times the pass-to-pass noise of stable land (~0.5 dB). Before comparing, we remove the drift of the quietest 30% of land (soil moisture, small calibration shifts).

③ Flooded field

patch ≥ 0.5 km², mean HH ≤ −12 dB, HH dropped ≥ HV drop − 1 dB

Paddies and fields never get as dark as a lake, and may not touch open water.

Why these: 0.5 km² ignores speckle-sized patches; −12 dB is darker than any dry field; HH falling as much as HV means a smooth water surface, not leaves lost at harvest (which drops HV first).

④ Water under trees

ΔHH ≥ +3 dB and |ΔHV| < 1.5 dB, within 3 km of water

Water under trunks makes a corner reflector (double bounce): HH jumps, HV hardly moves.

Why these: +3 dB = the returned power doubles, far above noise; HV steady rules out the canopy itself changing (growth, wind, rain on leaves). Also: vegetation that loses ≥ 3 dB of HV next to water. Only near water, so a wet forest far from any flood is not flagged.

⑤ Permanent water

dark on ≥ 90% of passes → removed

Lake core, river, sea: always water, so never "flood". An optical ocean mask is added.

Why 90%: one or two windy passes can make a lake look rough; 90% keeps those as permanent.

Optical teaches radar, at Tonle Sap. 9 cloud-free days gave −13.5 / −18.5 dB. Each day is scored with thresholds learned on the other days (leave-one-out), so the score is honest.

5 · Validation

How we checked it

Always against data we did not detect with: optical maps on clear days, another radar the same week, and ground gauges.

A · Clear-sky check · NASA OPERA DSWx-HLS (optical)
0.78F1 at Tonle Sap
(0.43 with fixed thresholds)

What: on days when Landsat or Sentinel-2 saw the site without clouds (within ±5 days of a NISAR pass), we compare NISAR water with NASA's optical water map, pixel by pixel, only where the optical view is clear.

How: F1 = balance of precision (is our water real?) and recall (did we find it?). Leave-one-out: each day is scored with thresholds learned only on the other days.

Also: detector v3 on a held-out track at the Inner Niger Delta: F1 0.70, vs 0.61 for the published method alone (Martinis et al. 2015).

Limit: optical sees only open water. It cannot check water under trees, which is exactly NISAR's addition.

B · Same-week radar · Canada's RCM (CSA, C-band)
79%of NISAR water also water in RCM
(IoU 0.65, 4 pairs)

What: at the Peace–Athabasca Delta (Canada) we paired 4 NISAR passes with RCM scenes taken ≤ 3 days apart and mapped water in each RCM scene from its own backscatter.

How: overlap of the two water maps on the same grid. A different satellite, agency and wavelength, so agreement is not an artefact of our data.

Under vegetation: where NISAR flags water under trees, RCM shows a double-bounce signal 42–45% of the time, vs 10% elsewhere (about 4×).

Limit: one delta, autumn 2026; C-band sees less under dense canopy, so it confirms the edges more than the core.

C · Ground truth · USGS EDEN gauges
0.95–1.00precision in the Everglades
(recall ≤ 0.35)

What: the Everglades water-depth network: gauge readings interpolated to a 400 m daily grid. Water = depth > 5 cm.

Result: what NISAR calls water is water. But it misses shallow water in dense sawgrass, so our areas are lower bounds.

History: RADARSAT-1 (CSA, 1998–2007) on the same grid shows the Mekong delta's flood pulse, ~100–350 km² dry vs ~1,500–2,800 km² wet. NISAR 2026 peak: 1,854 km².

6 · Limits

What we don't claim

Lower boundsShallow water in grass barely changes L-band. Read every number as "at least".
Candidates, not proofWater in the blank is a strong candidate. Flooded forest has no ground truth yet.
A sampleWettest pass per site, sites we picked. Not a global or average figure.
Provisional dataMost NISAR passes are still Provisional; calibration may change.
80 m gridGFM (20 m) and NISAR dates differ by ≤ 2 days. Small or fast floods blur.
